1. Title
Can Racial Preferences Predict the Decision to Pursue a Partner?
Subtitle:
Predicting “Yes” or “No” decisions in a speed-dating experiment

Visual: simple title slide, not a graph.
2. Introduction: Why study this?
Main points:
Romantic decisions are categorical but influenced by several personal and interpersonal factors.
People may state preferences before meeting someone, but stated preferences may not perfectly predict actual choices.
We investigate whether race-related preferences contain predictive information and how they compare with post-date impressions.
Key distinction:
Prediction is not causation, and predicting a choice is not the same as explaining why it occurred.

3. Research question
Primary question:
To what extent can an individual’s decision to pursue another person be predicted using their self-reported racial preference and the racial relationship between the two participants?

Secondary question:
How does race-related predictive power compare with models that also include post-date evaluations?

Define the target:
dec = Yes: participant wanted to see the partner again.
dec = No: participant did not.
4. Prior work and dataset origin
The dataset came from a Columbia University speed-dating experiment conducted across 21 waves from 2002–2004.
Participants:
Completed questionnaires before the event.
Met several partners for short dates.
Rated each partner after meeting.
Indicated whether they wanted to see each person again.
The associated paper is Fisman, Iyengar, Kamenica, and Simonson’s 2006 study, “Gender Differences in Mate Selection: Evidence from a Speed Dating Experiment,” published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics. Publication record
Important prior-work point:
The original research studied mate-selection behavior and differences between stated preferences and observed choices. Our project reframes the data as a supervised classification problem.

11. How the models were trained
Use a simple diagram:
528 participants
        ↓ split by iid
422 training participants ──→ Fit each model
106 test participants     ──→ Final evaluation
Important methodological choice:
Every decision made by a participant belongs entirely to either training or testing.

This prevents the same participant’s behavioral pattern from appearing in both sets.
Also mention:
Approximately 80% of participants were used for training.
Approximately 20% were held out for testing.
Classification threshold: 0.50.
All models were evaluated on the same 1,448 test decisions.
12. How the models were judged
Explain the metrics briefly:
Accuracy: overall percentage classified correctly.
Precision: among predicted “Yes” decisions, how many were actually “Yes”?
Recall: among actual “Yes” decisions, how many were detected?
F1: balance between precision and recall.
ROC-AUC: how well the model ranks “Yes” above “No” across thresholds.
Average precision: performance on the positive class relative to its prevalence.
Calibration: whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed rates.
All reported comparison metrics use one shared evaluation function.

Main conclusions:

The full models are substantially more accurate.

Logistic regression has the strongest accuracy, recall, and F1 on this test set.
The neural network does not improve performance despite being more complex.

16. Validation and possible application
Current validation:
Participant-level held-out test set.
Same cohort and evaluation method for every model.
ROC, precision-recall, confusion matrix, and calibration checks.

Realistic application:
Estimating the probability of a positive decision after a speed date, once the participant’s ratings are available.

Not supported:
Predicting attraction before two people interact.

The full models use post-date ratings, so their predictions are primarily post-interaction classifications.
17. Sources of error and bias
Include:
Sampling bias: Columbia-area volunteers are not representative of the broader population.
Historical context: data were collected from 2002–2004.
Small racial subgroups: some comparisons have high uncertainty.
Complete-case selection: removing missing observations may change the sample.
Measurement error: ratings and racial preferences are self-reported.
Outcome limitation: dec measures stated interest, not an actual relationship.
Target proximity: post-date ratings are recorded close to the decision and may partially restate the participant’s overall impression.
Simplified race categories: broad categories cannot represent the complexity of racial identity.
Single split uncertainty: results can vary with the selected participants.
Model-selection uncertainty: hyperparameters were not evaluated with nested cross-validation.
Interaction simplification: imprace × samerace is only one operational definition of racial-preference alignment.
18. Final conclusions and predictions
End with three direct answers:
Can we predict the decision?
Yes. Full models achieve ROC-AUC values around 0.83.

Can racial preference alone predict it?
Only weakly. The race-related benchmark has ROC-AUC 0.60 and recall 0.28.

What predicts the decision most strongly?
The participant’s impressions of the partner—especially attractiveness, fun, and shared interests—are substantially more informative.

Final statement:
Racial preference contributes some predictive signal, but it is not sufficient for strong individual-level prediction. Interpersonal evaluations after the date explain far more of the model’s predictive performance.
